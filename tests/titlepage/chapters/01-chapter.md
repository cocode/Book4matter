# Part One

## The Turning Dance

The rotary waltz turns continuously as the couple travels around the room, so
that a single bar of music carries the dancers through a full half-turn. Where
the hesitation waltz pauses and the cross-step waltz interrupts the rotation
with a walking figure, the rotary keeps spinning, and that unbroken turn is the
whole character of the dance.

This chapter exists mostly to give the fixture enough body text to flow across
several pages, so that a verso page carrying the book-title running head is
actually produced. That running head should read on a single line — the line
breaks the author placed in the title for the title page must not leak into it.
If "The Rotary Waltz" ever appears stacked across three lines in the head
margin, the newline-flattening has regressed.

Begin in closed position, weight on the standing foot, the working foot free.
The lead steps forward into the turn while the follow steps back, and both
dancers rotate a quarter turn over the first beat. The second and third beats
complete the half turn, so that the couple has exchanged places by the end of
the bar and stands ready to repeat the figure in the opposite direction.

Travel matters as much as rotation. A common fault is to spin in place, drilling
a hole in the floor while the rest of the room flows past. The rotary should
progress steadily along the line of dance, each turn carrying the couple a
little further around the room, the rotation and the travel braided together
into one smooth motion that never stops and never rushes.

Posture holds the figure together. The frame stays level, the heads counter the
turn, and the rise and fall is gentle rather than theatrical. When the rotation
is even and the travel is steady, the dance looks effortless from the outside,
which is precisely the effect a century of teachers have tried to pass along to
the next room full of beginners.

The history is tangled, as dance histories always are. The turning waltz
scandalised the ballrooms of the early nineteenth century precisely because of
this unbroken rotation and the closed hold it required, and the moral panic it
caused tells us more about the period than about the dance itself.

By the end of the century the rotary had been absorbed, tamed, and codified, and
it sat at the centre of social dancing until newer fashions crowded in. The
remainder of this chapter is ballast, present only to carry the text across
enough pages that both a verso and a recto running head are produced.

A six-by-nine page set at eleven points holds roughly three hundred and fifty
words, so a few hundred more will see us safely onto the following spread. The
words themselves need not say anything that matters; they need only occupy the
measure and turn the page.

Consider the music. A rotary waltz wants a steady three-four with a strong first
beat and an unhurried tempo, slow enough that the turn can breathe but quick
enough that the travel never stalls. Bands that understood dancers played the
rotary a touch under the concert tempo, and the floor thanked them for it.

Consider the floor. A crowded room turns the rotary into an exercise in
navigation: the couple must read the traffic ahead, ease the rotation to slip
through gaps, and never sacrifice the line of dance to a moment of showing off.
The best dancers make this negotiation invisible, and the worst make it everyone
else's problem.

Consider, finally, the partnership. The rotary is a conversation conducted
entirely through the frame: a lead offered, a weight accepted, a turn shared and
returned. Nothing is announced and nothing is forced. When it works, two people
who have never met can cross a ballroom as though they had rehearsed it, which
is the small everyday miracle the dance was built to produce.

That is enough ballast. If the page above this one carries a centred small-caps
line naming either the book or this chapter, on the correct side and on a single
line, the running head is behaving exactly as it should.
